02 / Privacy

You Can't Trust
A Server.

Every useful assistant needs your context, and every assistant that holds your context can leak it. Primnox's answer is architectural, not contractual — and as of v0.1.1 it is shipping.

Raw context stays on your machine. A small on-device model plus deterministic rules scrub it, and only de-identified text reaches the frontier model — which then gets rehydrated locally so you still read real names. Trust should come from code you can read, not a policy you have to believe.

● Live as of v0.1.1. Privacy Mirror scrubs PII on every cloud call — local models get raw text — and you still control exactly what context you share.

The
Manifesto.

We built Primnox because the useful version of this product kept being the one that knew too much about us.

Assistants get better the more context they have. That's not a design choice, it's the shape of the technology. The best scheduling advice comes from a model that has seen your calendar; the best writing help comes from one that has read your drafts. So the industry did the obvious thing: upload everything, and put a promise in the privacy policy.

A promise is not an architecture. It's a statement about intent, made by a company whose intent can change with a funding round, an acquisition, or a subpoena. It transfers risk to you and calls it trust.

The question isn't whether a company will behave. It's whether it is structurally capable of misbehaving.

Primnox is arranged so the answer is no, and since v0.1.1 that arrangement is running rather than promised. Raw context never leaves your machine. A small local model — one that does the boring, mechanical work of finding names, emails, tokens and addresses — rewrites your context into something de-identified. Deterministic rules run behind it, because a probabilistic scrubber that misses once has already failed. Only the scrubbed version travels.

The frontier model still does the hard thinking. It just does it about a person rather than about you specifically. When the answer comes back, your device puts the real details in. The intelligence is remote; the identity stays local.

The second thing: we wanted an assistant that isn't a coward.

Most assistants are optimised to be agreeable, which makes them useless at exactly the moments you need something else. Ask one whether you should drop out of university to go all-in on a two-month-old side project and it will help you write the announcement.

Primnox pushes back. Not about small things — it isn't going to lecture you for staying out on a Tuesday. About real ones. It will tell you when a plan is adrenaline rather than a signal, and it will say so in a way that sounds like a friend rather than a compliance department.

It also roasts you if you insult it, and apologises later. That isn't a gimmick. An assistant you talk to every day needs a shape you can push against. A tool that agrees with everything isn't a friend, it's a mirror — and nobody takes advice from a mirror.

It will never guilt you, pressure you, or hold your unfinished tasks over your head. Whatever an assistant is for, that isn't it.

Both halves are the same bet: that the version of this product worth using every day is the one that has your context and hasn't taken anything from you to get it.

— The Primnox team
